Part One

How to Submit Your Poetry

Submitting poetry is a craft of its own. The poems matter most, but editors also notice whether a writer follows guidelines, writes a clean cover letter, and behaves professionally. None of it is difficult — it simply rewards patience and a little organization.

01

Before You Send

  • Revise, then rest — read finished poems aloud after a week.
  • Assemble a batch of three to five poems that share a voice.
  • Lead with your strongest poem; editors decide early.
  • Keep a master file and a status spreadsheet.
02

Choosing Where to Submit

  • Read an issue first — fit matters more than prestige.
  • Check the reading period before submitting.
  • Note the fee; many fine journals are free.
  • Respect eligibility — some contests favor newer poets.
03

Formatting Your Poems

  • One document, one poem per page, page breaks between.
  • Readable 12-point serif, single-spaced within stanzas.
  • Title on each page; remove your name if reading is blind.
  • Send a PDF to protect unusual lineation.
05

Simultaneous Subs & Etiquette

  • Submit to several journals at once unless forbidden.
  • Withdraw everywhere the moment a poem is accepted.
  • Don't query before the stated response time.
  • Check each journal's definition of "published."
06

Using Submittable

  • Free for writers — one account tracks everything.
  • Any fee shown belongs to the journal, not the platform.
  • Follow each form's exact instructions.
  • Withdraw from the dashboard when accepted elsewhere.
08

Handling Responses

  • Expect rejection — it's about fit, not worth.
  • Take "please send again" seriously; it's real encouragement.
  • Read the contract; note rights and reprint terms.
  • Submit steadily to build a publication record.

04  ·  Writing the Cover Letter

A cover letter is short, warm, and professional — three or four sentences. It does not explain or defend the poems. A reliable template:

Dear Editors,

Please consider the enclosed poems, "[Title One]," "[Title Two]," and "[Title Three]," for publication in [Journal Name].

[One sentence of bio: recent publications, or simply that this would be among your first. If you have none, say so plainly — editors publish new writers every issue.]

Thank you for your time and for the work you do. I look forward to hearing from you.

Warmly,
[Your Name]

Do not apologize, describe your themes, list every credit, or address editors by the wrong journal name. Always double-check the greeting.

Before You Begin

What Every New Poet Should Know

A few realities of the publishing world will save you confusion, disappointment, and money. Read these once before you send anything.

Rights & What Editors Actually Buy

When a journal accepts a poem, it does not own it forever. Most acquire First Serial Rights (also called First North American Serial Rights) — the right to be the first to publish it. After publication, those rights return to you, and you keep the copyright throughout. You are then free to include the poem in a future book or, once it counts as a reprint, submit it to venues that accept previously published work. Always read the acceptance note to confirm what rights are taken and when they revert.

“Published” Is Broader Than You Think

Nearly all journals want unpublished poems, and many define “published” to include your personal blog, a public social-media post, a contest anthology, or even a poem posted in an online workshop. If a poem has appeared anywhere public, treat it as a reprint and look specifically for journals that accept reprints. When in doubt, ask the editor before submitting.

Reading Fees — and the No-Fee Philosophy

Some journals charge a small fee (often $2–$3) to submit, usually to cover the cost of their submission platform. This is normal and not a scam. But you never need to pay to build a serious publication record: the majority of respected journals are free, and many paying journals offer fee waivers on request. Reserve paid submissions and contest fees for venues you genuinely want. A good rule: if a journal charges more than a few dollars and does not pay contributors or offer something concrete in return, be skeptical.

Avoiding Vanity Presses & Scams

Legitimate journals and contests never ask you to buy your way into print. Watch for these red flags:

“You’ve been selected!”

Unsolicited emails congratulating you and then asking you to buy an expensive anthology or “certificate” to see your poem in print. Real acceptances don’t require a purchase.

Everyone wins

“Contests” that accept virtually everyone and profit by selling the anthology back to contributors. A real contest has genuine selection and named judges.

Rights grabs

Terms that claim all rights or ownership of your work in perpetuity. Legitimate journals take first or one-time rights, then return them.

High fees, no pay

Large submission or “editing” fees with no payment to writers and no track record. Check a venue’s reputation before paying anything.

When unsure, look a venue up on Poets & Writers, check whether respected poets have published there, and search Writer Beware for warnings.

Payment: What to Expect

Poetry rarely pays much, and many fine journals pay in contributor copies or exposure rather than cash. Those that pay might offer anywhere from a token amount to $50–$100 or more per poem (Rattle and Poetry are among the more generous). Publishing for little or no pay is normal early on; the value is in readership, credits, and building toward a book.

Individual Poems vs. Chapbooks vs. Full Collections

Most poets build in stages. Start by placing individual poems in journals. Once you have a cluster of related, often previously published poems, you can assemble a chapbook (a short collection, usually 15–30 pages) and enter chapbook contests or submit to small presses. A full-length collection (typically 48–80+ pages) usually comes later, and having journal credits and a chapbook strengthens that manuscript’s chances.

Patience & Persistence

Response times commonly run one to six months, and rejection is the norm even for accomplished poets — acceptance rates at good journals are often under five percent. A “no” usually means the poem didn’t fit that issue, not that it lacks worth. Keep a steady rhythm of submissions, celebrate personal notes from editors, and treat each rejection as one step closer to the right home for the poem.

A Guided Walkthrough

Your First Submission — Step by Step

If you have never submitted poems to a journal before, this section is for you. It is a single evening’s work — start to finish — and it will teach your hands the whole choreography that carries poems from your desk into the world. Do it once, and every submission after this becomes ordinary.

01

Choose a packet of poems

  • Most journals ask for three to five poems in a single submission. Pick four.
  • Not your favorite four. The four that stand together as a small suite — a shared music, a shared attention.
  • Each poem should be finished — not perfect, but ended.
  • Each has been revised at least twice and rested at least a week.
02

Read every poem aloud

  • Read each poem slowly, from title to final line.
  • Mark anywhere your voice catches, hurries, or falters. Trust that.
  • Fix only what the reading exposed — do not begin a wider revision tonight.
  • Save the packet as Packet_YourName_final.docx.
03

Format the packet

  • One poem per page. 12-point serif, single-spaced within stanzas, one-inch margins.
  • Poem title on line one; stanzas below in your intended lineation. Preserve line breaks exactly.
  • Page one top-left: your name, address, email, phone.
  • Every page: running header with Your Last Name / poem title / page #.
  • For long poems, use consecutive pages with the same running head.
04

Pick three journals

  • Open the journals table below. Pick three whose poetry you admire.
  • Click each link and confirm the reading period is open tonight.
  • Confirm they accept the number of poems in your packet.
  • Prefer free journals for a first submission. Paid contests come later.
05

Write the cover letter

  • Use the template above. Three or four sentences.
  • Change the journal name and packet contents for each submission.
  • If you have no publications yet, say so plainly — editors publish new poets every issue.
  • Read the greeting twice. Wrong journal name is the fastest way to bury a submission.
06

Submit on Submittable

  • Make a free Submittable account if you do not have one.
  • Follow each journal’s link from the table — it usually opens the submission form.
  • Upload the .docx or paste the packet, paste in the cover letter, submit.
  • The whole trio takes under twenty minutes once formatted.
07

Log every send

  • Open a plain spreadsheet — Google Sheets, Excel, or a notebook page.
  • Columns: Poems · Journal · Date Sent · Response Deadline · Status · Notes.
  • Log the poem titles individually so you can track which have been placed.
  • Set a calendar reminder ninety days out to check status.
08

Start the next poems

  • The packet is out. That part of the work is done.
  • Return to your desk tomorrow and open the next draft.
  • Submitting is not a project — it is a habit. Write. Send. Write again.
  • The rhythm is what turns a writer into a published poet.

The whole walkthrough, done in one sitting, is the most valuable exercise in this guide. After tonight, submitting is no longer a threshold — it is a door you have already walked through.

Part Three · What Comes Back

The Rejection & Revision Playbook

Most of what returns from a poetry submission is not an acceptance. Poetry has an especially long tail: a poem may sit in editorial queues for six months, come back with nothing, and then be accepted at the fifteenth journal that reads it. Learning to read what you receive — and to answer it well, or not answer at all — is a working poet’s most useful skill.

The Three Kinds of Rejection

01

The Form Rejection

  • Same paragraph every poet receives. No named reader, no note about your poems.
  • Reads: “Thank you for sending. Unfortunately these are not right for us. We wish you the best of luck placing them.”
  • This means only one thing: the poems did not fit that issue.
  • Not a verdict on the poems. Send them out again the same day if you can.
02

The Tiered Rejection

  • A form rejection with one added line: “This came close,” or “We enjoyed reading your work.”
  • Sometimes signed by a name, sometimes by an editorial team.
  • Meaning: your packet made it past the first reader. It reached a discussion.
  • Response: no reply needed. Log it, celebrate quietly, send that packet out again immediately.
03

The Personal Rejection

  • Written specifically about your poems, by name or by title. Two to six sentences.
  • May name a specific poem, an image, a line. May say “please send us more soon.”
  • The rarest and most valuable reply in this guide — under five percent of submissions receive one.
  • Response: yes. Reply briefly, warmly, and follow through — see the template below.

What a Personal “Please Send Again” Actually Means

When an editor takes fifteen minutes to write to you personally, they are telling you three things at once: they read the packet carefully, they liked your work, and they want to see more of it. In poetry, editors are also signaling that your voice and your line feel worth returning to. The invitation is real, not a courtesy. Treat it as an open door and walk through it.

Reliable reply to a personal rejection

Dear [Editor’s Name],

Thank you for reading my packet so closely, and for the kind note about “[Poem Title].” It means a great deal.

I would very much like to send another packet. I will submit within the next few weeks through your regular submission process, and I will mention this note in the cover letter so you can find it.

Thank you again — I appreciate the invitation.

Warmly,
[Your Name]

Then do it. Submit another packet within four to six weeks, through the normal channel, and reference this reply in your new cover letter: “[Editor] kindly wrote to me about my last packet, especially ‘[Previous Poem],’ and asked me to send more work.” That single sentence lifts your submission out of the general pile.

When to Revise, When Not To

Revise a poem when…

  • Three or more personal rejections all name the same problem — the ending, the closing image, a particular stanza.
  • Reading the poem aloud tonight, you can hear the flaw yourself — a line that lands wrong, a metaphor that overreaches.
  • You have grown as a poet since drafting it and can now see what you missed.
  • A poem has been in fifteen or more packets without one personal note.

Do not revise when…

  • You received a single form rejection. Send the packet again as-is.
  • An editor’s note praises the poems overall and names one small preference (a taste call).
  • You are tempted to change the opening because it just came back from journal #3. Give it more time.
  • You are rewriting from anxiety rather than from craft. Rest first, then decide.

Repacking the Packet

Between rejections, do not only re-send the same four poems. Rotate. A packet that comes back three times has usually been read by editors who share taste — try a different combination. Take out the strongest poem and pair it with two new drafts. Or split the packet: send the two most connected poems to one journal, the two most independent to another. Movement is the point.

The Fifteen-Sub Rule

A working benchmark for emerging poets: if a poem has been in fifteen carefully chosen packets and comes back with only form rejections and no personal notes, retire it to a folder and let it rest at least six months before revising. If it returns even one personal rejection along the way, keep sending — that poem is finding its readers, just slowly. Poems, more than any other form, sometimes need years to find their journal.

Rejection is the ordinary weather of a working poet’s life. It is not a verdict on your poems or your voice. It is the room where the work gets stronger.

Part Four · The Life After Publication

The Afterlife of a Poem

A poem does not end when it appears in a journal. Poetry has more paths into a second life than any other form: individual poems can be reprinted directly by journals that welcome previously published work, featured on daily-poem services like Poetry Daily and Verse Daily, nominated for annual prizes and anthologies, gathered into your own chapbook or full-length collection, and turned into new work through translation and adaptation. This afterlife is where a poet’s early credits become a working body of work.

What Counts as a Reprint

Once a poem has appeared in print or online anywhere — a literary journal, an anthology, a Substack post, even your own website — most journals consider it published. In poetry, most journals accept only unpublished work; a smaller number explicitly accept reprints if you disclose prior publication. When sending a poem again:

Confirm rights have reverted

  • Most poetry journals take First Serial Rights only, which revert to you on publication.
  • Read your original acceptance letter to be sure.
  • If unclear, email the original journal and ask when rights revert. Save their reply.

Disclose in the cover letter

  • One sentence: “‘[Poem Title]’ first appeared in [Original Journal], [Season/Year].”
  • Include a URL if the poem is online.
  • Never hide prior publication. Reprint-friendly venues expect and welcome the disclosure.

Send only to reprint-friendly venues

  • Most journals accept only unpublished work. Do not submit reprints there.
  • The venues listed below explicitly accept reprints as of August 4, 2026, or run features that pull from prior journal publication.
  • Always confirm the venue’s current guidelines before submitting.

Update your master list

  • Tag each poem: unpublished, published, or reprint-eligible.
  • Rotate reprints just as you rotate unpublished poems — new venues, fresh cycles.

Always check the venue’s current guidelines rather than an older list.

From Poems to Chapbook to Book

The purpose of placing poems is not only to reach new readers, though that matters. It is also to build the foundation for a chapbook and eventually a full-length collection. For emerging poets, individual publications serve two distinct paths:

The chapbook. Fifteen to thirty poems, arranged in a single arc or preoccupation, become a strong chapbook manuscript. Contests like Frontier Debut Chapbook, Rattle Chapbook, Two Sylvias, and Bull City Press run annual chapbook prizes that publish first books for poets with a scattering of journal credits.

The full collection. Sixty to eighty poems can become a full-length book. Major first-book prizes (Yale Younger Poets, Cave Canem, APR/Honickman, National Poetry Series, Autumn House) all expect a manuscript in which most poems have appeared in journals. Every poem you place is a small argument for the book’s readiness.

Every reprint acceptance, every Poetry Daily feature, every Best of the Net nomination is another brick in that foundation. Publish steadily, keep track of every credit, and over the years an ordinary poem accumulates a life far larger than its first appearance.

A reprint is not a lesser publication. It is a poem continuing to do its work.

Sending more than one separate batch to the same journal in one reading period. Usually discouraged — send one batch and wait.
First Serial Rights
The right to be the first to publish a poem. Reverts to you after publication; you keep the copyright.
Reprint
A poem published before (including on a blog or social media). Only certain journals accept reprints — check first.
Reading period
The window when a journal accepts submissions. Many open only a few months or weeks a year.
Simultaneous / rolling
“Rolling” means always open; submissions are read as they arrive rather than in fixed windows.
Cover letter
A brief, professional note accompanying your poems — not a place to explain them.
Contributor copies
Free copies of the issue your work appears in, often given in place of (or alongside) payment.
Chapbook
A short poetry collection, usually 15–30 pages.
Full-length collection
A complete poetry book, typically 48–80+ pages.
Slush pile
The stack of unsolicited submissions editors read through. Yours starts here — that’s normal.
Tiered / expedited response
An optional paid upgrade some journals offer for a faster reply. Never required.
Fee waiver
A free pass around a submission fee, offered by many journals on request or for writers facing financial barriers.
Nom de plume / pen name
A pseudonym. Allowed by most journals — note it in your cover letter if you use one.
Get Started

A Simple Starting Plan

If this feels like a lot, start here. This four-step rhythm will place you on a professional footing within a single afternoon.

1

Pick three free journals whose work you admire and whose reading period is open now.

2

Prepare one clean batch of three to five poems, best poem first, in a single document.

3

Write one short cover letter using the template above, tailoring the journal name for each.

4

Log each submission in a spreadsheet or free tracker, then set a reminder to submit again next month.

Poems live when they find readers. Send them out, keep good records, and let each "no" move you toward the right "yes."

From the Publisher

A Personal Note to You

First, let me say how glad I am that you are here, and how much your writing has come to mean to me. Whatever happens with any single submission, please know that I believe in you and in the work you are doing.

I want to tell you something before you begin, because I wish someone had told me plainly when I was starting out: this was hard for me too. For years I sent my work into the quiet and waited, and what came back, again and again, was no. The rejections arrived in bundles — some gentle, most simply silent — and every one of them asked whether I was willing to keep going. I decided that I was. Not because I felt sure of the work, but because I had come to believe that the writers who make it are rarely the most gifted in the room; they are the ones who keep sending, who let each no be a comma and never a period.

What carried me through was not confidence — it was persistence, and the quiet discipline of returning to the desk: revise, submit, write down the date, start the next poem, and send again. And I promise you this: the acceptances, when they finally came, did not cancel the rejections. They grew out of them. Every no was teaching me something, even when it didn’t feel that way.

I care very much that you get published — not someday, but truly, and not because publication is the only measure of a writer, but because your work deserves to be read, and because I know how much it means to hold that first acceptance in your hands. So I’ll ask of you the same thing I once had to ask of myself, and I’ll ask it gently: choose a poem you believe in, find two or three journals from the lists in this guide, and submit it today. Not tomorrow. Not when it feels perfect — it never will. Today. The waiting is part of the work, and you are more ready than you think.

And let me say this plainly, because I mean it: I think of you as my peers — fellow writers, not students beneath me. I respect every one of you. It has been one of the great joys of my life to witness your talent up close and to sit with you in conversation about writing and literature, and I have come away from those hours happier and richer for them. I am confident — genuinely, not politely — that you will be published if you persist.

One last thing. Other writers were generous with me when I was starting out, and that generosity is a debt I can only repay forward — so on the pages that follow, I’ve shared a little of my own work with you: what I believe about writing, and two introductions from books I’m still making. They are not offered as the best of anything, only as honest examples, one writer to another.

I’m rooting for you — always. Be gentle with yourself, keep going, and know that I am in your corner every step of the way.

— James Mulhern, Silver Current Press

A Gift, One Writer to Another

An Artistic Creed

Over the years, so many writers generously shared their work with me — their drafts, their beliefs, their hard-won lessons — and I would not be where I am without that generosity. So let me share some of mine with you in return. I do not offer these because I think they are the best; I don’t. I offer them simply as honest examples you can look at, learn from, and then set aside to write something that sounds like no one but you. Here is one of mine — a statement of what I believe about my own work.

I write as a Transcendentalist — in sensibility and in spirit. I came to this not as a doctrine to be argued but as a way of seeing I could not put down. Emerson taught me to trust the inner light and to call the great current of things the Oversoul. Thoreau taught me to look long and closely at the near and the small until they open. Whitman taught me that a single life, honestly told, contains multitudes and belongs to everyone. I am in their debt, and I do not pretend otherwise.

I believe the sacred is not far off but here — in a kitchen, a sickroom, a stretch of shoreline, the face of someone loved and lost. I believe the soul is not sealed within us but joined, through the Oversoul, to every other soul that has been or will be. And I believe that nothing true is ever finished. The Oversoul repeats and refines what it has made, never completing it, and I take my instruction from that unhurried, unfinished work.

This is why I return, deliberately, to the same scenes, the same symbols, the same kinds of people. The repetitions in my work are purposeful — they are my way of pointing to the truth I hold. I am not repeating myself; I am practicing recurrence, gathering up the same enduring truths and setting them down again in new hands, new voices, new incarnations. What is laid down is taken up. Nothing we love is lost; like water, like breath, like light, it is only changed, only carried forward, only lived anew.

So I write from mercy rather than judgment, and I extend that mercy to every character I make. I treat each story as a small act of faith — a way of summoning what is unseen and holding it in the light a little longer. If my work has one purpose, it is this: to show that the ordinary is holy, that memory is a form of love, and that we are, all of us, one people — gathered, remade, and never truly parted.

— James F. Mulhern

From My Own Desk — Works in Progress

Two Sample Introductions

In the same generous spirit — the way other writers once shared their pages with me — I want to hand you two more of mine. Many books open with an introduction or a foreword, and writing one for your own work is harder than it looks. Please don’t take these as the best examples; they are simply mine, and I offer them humbly. And I want you to see them honestly, as works in progress: both books are still being made as I write this, and I am still revising these very pages. I cross out, begin again, and doubt them, exactly as you will with yours. That is not a sign you are failing. It is the work. I share them only so you have something real to look at when the time comes to write your own.

Introduction — All We Ever Needed (a story collection)

The stories in All We Ever Needed move through kitchens, classrooms, beaches, bookstores, apartments, and fading neighborhoods where love and damage rarely arrive one at a time. Again and again, people try to live inside family histories that have already marked them. Some are buoyed by memory, some haunted by it, and some use wit, cruelty, appetite, or make-believe to keep from being swallowed by what they know.

A few images recur by design. Water is the constant: a seawater cure sought on the Feast of the Assumption, a pond that has kept its drowned, a bath drawn for an aging mother, a fountain, a river, and at last a rain that leaves a man drenched to the bone. I have let it move through the book as more than weather. Water is the oldest sign of the spiritual life — the water of baptism, of ruin and renewal — and it runs beneath these ordinary lives as the possibility, never quite promised, of being made clean.

I take language to be a sacred inheritance and the act of naming to be a kind of devotion. These stories are my prayers, and my prayers are my stories; the recurrences in these pages are laid there in that spirit. What remains, after the jokes sour and the illusions fall away, is the question the collection keeps returning to: what, in the end, sustains us? The answers here are partial, unruly, and deeply human.

— James Mulhern  (draft · in progress)

Foreword — Mia Bambina and Other Stories

There is a durable American lineage of fiction that begins not with spectacle but with a room, a family car, a parish, a school hallway, a kitchen table at which someone says the wrong thing and the air changes. One hears, at moments in these stories, the grave attentiveness of Andre Dubus, the domestic acuity of Alice McDermott, the religious pressure of Mary Gordon, the rueful comic steadiness of Richard Russo.

The five stories gathered in Mia Bambina and Other Stories move through Boston dormitories and beaches, old kitchens and classrooms, South Florida cafés and storefronts heavy with incense. Their geography is specific, but the territory is interior: the strange compromise by which people continue to live near shame, grief, lust, resentment, love. The sacred and the profane are not opposites here. They are neighbors. In certain families, they are sisters.

These are stories of mortality, memory, and moral compromise told without sentimentality. They ask how much harm can be done in the name of love, how much mercy can survive in a damaged person, and whether any ritual can prepare us for the final silence. The answer, mercifully, is not simplified. The stories look hard. They do not look away.

— from the front matter of Mia Bambina and Other Stories  (draft · in progress)
